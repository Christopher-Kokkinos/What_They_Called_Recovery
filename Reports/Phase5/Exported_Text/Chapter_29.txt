CHAPTER 29 — The Mirror

Sunday began with a problem Zoe had not expected.

Nothing useful was open.

Petrol stations were open.

Supermarkets were open.

A garden centre advertised breakfast until eleven.

But the places that answered questions about old companies and old paperwork had apparently decided organised crime could wait until Monday.

Zoe stood beside the beige estate outside a retail park and looked at the dark windows of an office-supply store.

“This country shuts down whenever we need stationery.”

Elias locked the car.

“You have three notebooks.”

“Two.”

“Three.”

“The small one doesn’t count.”

“It contains writing.”

“It contains bus times.”

“Still a notebook.”

Zoe started towards the supermarket.

“I need coloured tabs.”

“For?”

“The mirror.”

Elias looked at her.

“That explanation clarified nothing.”

“Good.”

Inside, Zoe bought sticky tabs, two pens, a ruler, a pack of index cards and chocolate biscuits.

Elias looked into the basket.

“The biscuits are operational?”

“Morale.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“It is if morale is low.”

He added batteries.

Zoe looked at them.

“Operational?”

“Yes.”

“Boring.”

They paid cash.

Outside, rain had not started yet.

That felt suspicious.

* * *

The garden-centre cafe had artificial waterfalls, discounted patio furniture and an elderly couple quietly disagreeing about hydrangeas.

Zoe ate toast.

Elias drank coffee.

Again.

Always coffee.

She put three index cards on the table.

On the first she wrote:

ASK

On the second:

WATCH

On the third:

DON’T GUESS

Elias looked at them.

“Better.”

“Than what?”

“Nothing.”

“Good answer.”

She put a blue tab on ASK because she had bought blue tabs and intended to use them.

“What do we ask?”

“One narrow thing.”

“Adult-facing.”

“Preferably.”

“No child name.”

“Correct.”

“No current address.”

“Correct.”

“No medical.”

“Correct.”

“No accusation.”

Elias lifted his coffee.

“You have remembered the rule.”

“I wrote the rule.”

“You wrote several rules.”

“Because adults keep needing them.”

He let that pass.

Zoe opened the intermediary sheet from Voss and Vale.

Payment intermediary.

Communications intermediary.

Vehicle supplier.

Document certifier.

Two addresses.

Three companies.

One company appeared twice.

Meridian Placement Services Ltd.

It sat above a transfer certification in Zoe’s folder.

It also appeared in Voss and Vale’s old paperwork as a document processor and, later, as the commissioning route for at least one recovery that no longer looked legitimate.

But Meridian did not appear plainly by name in the visible index on the drive.

Only codes did.

Zoe tapped the company name.

“Meridian.”

Elias did not immediately agree.

“Why?”

“Because it touches paperwork.”

“That isn’t enough.”

“It appears in my folder.”

“Yes.”

“And in Voss’s jobs.”

“Yes.”

“So it connects two parts without needing my name.”

Elias looked at the sheet.

“What would you ask?”

Zoe thought.

Not whether Meridian trafficked children.

Not whether Zoe’s adoption was fake.

Not what BRG meant.

Something small.

Something an ordinary company should be able to answer without caring who asked.

“Whether one of their documents is actually theirs.”

Elias looked up.

“Which one?”

“Not mine.”

“Good.”

“One Voss already has.”

“Why?”

“Because if Meridian reacts, he can see things we can’t.”

That survived.

Elias sat back.

“Then we need a document reference that doesn’t identify a child.”

“And a way to ask them.”

“And a way to know whether their answer is unusual.”

Zoe frowned. “How?”

“We compare it with something known to be ordinary.”

She reached for a biscuit and stopped. “Voss might have one.”

“He might.”

She pushed the biscuit onto Elias’s saucer. “Bribe.”

“I don’t take bribes.”

“Everyone says that before the biscuit.”

He ate it. “Corruption.”

“Continue.”

* * *

Contacting Voss and Vale was the first problem. They had no standing line. That had been intentional.

Reyes’s phone was gone.

The active contractor channels had been killed.

The intermediary sheet mapped places where contact had once happened, but Zoe did not want any of those.

“Can they find us?” she asked.

“If we make enough noise.”

“That defeats the point.”

“Usually.”

“Can we find them?”

“Possibly.”

“You hate that word.”

“I use it accurately.”

“Still hate it.”

Elias looked through the cafe window.

Rain had finally started.

“Voss kept one passive watch channel.”

“He told us?”

“He implied it.”

“When?”

“After Clock Square.”

Zoe thought back.

“He said he’d know if Mac tried to revive contractor traffic.”

“Which means he’s watching something.”

“Yes.”

“Can we put something into it?”

“Not from here.”

“Then somewhere else.”

Elias watched her.

“You’re about to turn an old contractor channel into a message board.”

“Not a message board.”

“What would you send?”

She already knew what she wanted.

Not a sentence.

Not a location.

Not a name.

One fragment they would recognise.

Then a time.

“MPS-17.”

Elias said nothing.

“Then fourteen hundred.”

“Too ambiguous.”

“Good.”

“He might ignore it.”

“Then we learn something.”

“This is not a test of Voss.”

“It can tell us something about him without being the reason we do it.”

That was different enough that Elias considered it.

“What does he gain?”

“Nothing.”

“What could he sell?”

“A company name he already knows.”

“What could he expose?”

“That somebody is checking an old document.”

“And if he tells Mac?”

“Mac might react.”

Elias looked at her.

Zoe held his gaze.

“That’s the mirror.”

He did not agree immediately.

Good.

Agreement that came too easily was not worth much anymore.

Finally he said, “We use a location we do not reuse.”

“Fine.”

“A route that cannot lead back to the hotel.”

“Fine.”

“We send nothing except the fragment and time.”

“Fine.”

“And if I say move -”

“I move.”

The emergency rule.

No argument first.

Explanation once they were moving.

Elias finished his coffee.

The biscuit was gone.

Bribe successful.

Voss looked at her.

“If they’re testing one, we need something normal beside it.”

He nodded.

Now the test belonged to more than one person.

“You think he got it?”

“Yes.”

“Understood it?”

“Probably.”

“Then ambiguity has limits.”

“Annoyingly reasonable.”

At one forty-five they entered a public library in a different town.

Different building.

Different network.

“It doesn’t ask for a child’s name.”

Elias checked the rest.

“Or a case name.”

“Good.”

They had chosen the reference Voss already possessed.

MPS-17-4408

Authenticated-copy certification.

No child identifier.

No address.

At one fifty-eight, Zoe opened a fresh notebook page.

She wrote the question in ordinary words first:

Does Meridian confirm MPS-17-4408 as an authenticated-copy reference?

No allegation.

No explanation.

No hint that they possessed anything else.

The return address belonged to a fresh account created for this one purpose.

At two exactly, Zoe submitted it.

Closed the browser.

Logged out.

Stood.

“Done.”

Elias said, “Move.”

She moved.

No argument.

They left by the front entrance, turned right, crossed at the lights, entered a pharmacy and left through the other side into a service lane.

No one followed.

At the beige estate, Elias unlocked the car only when they were close.

He drove.

Three turns later:

“Nothing obvious.”

Zoe looked at him.

“Explanation delivered.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

She put a yellow tab on WATCH.

Nothing to write under it yet.

* * *

“Too quick for someone to find an old paper file.”

“Probably automated intake.”

“So it tells us nothing yet.”

“Correct.”

She did not like that.

She accepted it anyway.

The public switchboard.

MPS-09-2281

Brief.

Still noticeable.

Hold music.

Then the line disconnected.

Vale looked at the phone.

“Accident?”

“No.”

“Call back?”

“No.”

She hated waiting.

Which was probably why it was correct.
