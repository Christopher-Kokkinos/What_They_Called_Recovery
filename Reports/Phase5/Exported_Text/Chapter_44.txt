CHAPTER 44 — Loyalty Fracture

Sunday morning, Voss signed his name.

He stared at it afterward.

Not because the signature looked wrong.

Because it looked ordinary.

Black ink.

Two words.

The same signature he had put on invoices, vehicle leases, contractor agreements, expense claims and Christmas cards he never wanted to write.

Now it sat beneath seventeen pages explaining how his team had moved a child from a legitimate temporary placement into a private receiving household using authority that the issuing institution said it had never created.

Vale stood beside him.

“You done?”

“No.”

“You signed.”

“That isn’t the same thing.”

She took the pen.

Read the final declaration.

Again.

Then signed beneath him.

Her handwriting was smaller.

Neater.

Voss looked at it.

Vale said:

“Don’t.”

“What?”

“Comment.”

“I wasn’t.”

“You were going to.”

“No.”

“Good.”

Priya Dey sat across the table.

Sunday access to the office.

No reception staff.

No one else.

Three copies of the contractor statement lay in front of her.

One for Priya.

One for independent counsel working with the institutional review.

One sealed for Voss and Vale.

No child names.

No current addresses.

No sibling information.

No guess presented as fact.

The yellow-coat case existed only as a historic identifier and a sequence of actions they could personally support.

Priya turned to the declaration page.

“Before I witness these, I need to ask you both again. Are you signing voluntarily?”

“Yes,” Voss said.

Vale nodded.

“Yes.”

“Have I promised either of you immunity?”

“No.”

“Have I told you this guarantees favourable treatment?”

“No.”

“Have either of you been offered something in return?”

Voss looked at Vale.

Then back at Priya.

“Legacy counsel offered a favourable characterisation if we surrendered retained material.”

“Did you accept?”

“No.”

“Why?”

He thought about it.

“Because I stopped trusting the people offering it.”

Priya turned to Vale.

“Same?”

Vale hesitated.

“Not exactly.”

Voss looked at her.

She ignored him. “I didn’t want them writing it for me.”

Priya waited. Vale looked at the statement beneath her signature. “I was there.”

“Yes.”

“I want my part to say what I did.”

Priya wrote that on her interview pad. Separate from the statement. Then she witnessed both signatures. No forgiveness. No deal. Just their names beneath their own account.

At nine twenty, Zoe spoke to Ruth Ellison. Elias was already outside the room. Zoe had asked him to leave before Ruth had even mentioned it. Ruth noticed. Didn’t comment. Good. “I’ve received some adult-side records that may relate to you,” she said. “What records?”

“Voss and Vale’s signed statements. A sponsor agreement supplied through separate counsel. And confirmation that North Vale and Westmere are preserving a restricted group of historic cases.”

“Good.”

Zoe looked at the wall.

Of course it did.

“Mac?”

“Yes.”

“His real name on it?”

“Yes.”

“Signature?”

“Yes.”

“It came through counsel.”

“Why?”

“I don’t know.”

Good answer.

Ruth continued.

“North Vale and Westmere now have records of their own. Priya has the contractor statements. Mac’s agreement exists independently of your copy.”

Zoe rubbed her thumb against the side of the chair.

The backpack was beside the bed. The drive inside it. Still important. No longer alone. “Don’t put everything together yet.”

Ruth tilted her head. “Why?”

“I don’t want one person holding the only complete version.”

“That may change once investigators require material.”

“I know.”

“But for now, the sources can remain separately preserved.”

“Yes.”

“Child-specific material stays restricted.”

“Yes.”

Zoe nodded. “That.”

Ruth paused. “I also need to tell you something about Helen and Brian.”

Zoe’s shoulders tightened. “What?”

“They’ve instructed their own solicitor.”

“Mac’s?”

“No.”

That landed harder than Zoe expected.

“Their solicitor contacted the safeguarding review. They have agreed to separate voluntary interviews.”

“Separate?”

“To protect the independence of what they remember.”

Zoe looked towards the closed door.

“What are they saying?”

“I don’t know yet.”

“Will I get to see it?”

“If it becomes relevant to your legal position and disclosure is appropriate.”

Zoe almost smiled.

“Appropriate.”

“Yes.”

“Very solicitor.”

“I’m afraid so.”

* * *

Helen and Brian sat in separate rooms.

Their solicitor had suggested it.

Helen hated the idea.

Then agreed.

Brian hated it more.

He agreed too.

Neutral office.

Audio recording.

Two safeguarding investigators.

Their own solicitor available.

No police interview.

Not yet.

That word had been explained carefully.

They could stop.

They could seek criminal advice.

Nothing they said came with immunity.

Helen had slept perhaps three hours.

Rachel Moore sat opposite her.

Recorder between them.

“How long has Zoe lived with you?” “Five years.”

“Do you consider yourself her mother?”

Helen’s throat tightened.

“Yes.”

“Legally?”

She looked at the recorder.

Then Rachel.

“No.”

Nobody reacted.

That made it worse.

“When did you understand that the route by which Zoe came to you was not an ordinary lawful adoption?”

Helen thought of the observation room.

Mac.

Brian saying they should leave.

Her own phone call the following morning.

“Before we accepted her.”

“What did you understand at that point?”

“Not everything.”

“Tell me what you did understand.”

Helen folded her hands.

“The normal adoption process didn’t apply.”

Rachel waited.

“I knew records were going to change.”

“What else?”

“That whatever was happening with Zoe’s original family wasn’t finished.”

“Did anyone tell you her parents were dead?”

“No.”

“Did anyone tell you they had relinquished her?”

“No.”

“Did you believe a court had lawfully transferred parental responsibility to you?”

Helen stared at her hands.

“No.”

Rachel let the silence stay. “Why did you proceed?”

There were too many answers. Grief. The daughter they had lost. Infertility. The adoption rejection. The little girl behind glass. Helen chose the ugliest one because it was probably the truest. “I told myself we could give her a better life.”

“Better than what?”

“Whatever she was going back to.”

“Did you know what she was going back to?”

“No.”

“What did you know about her original family?”

“Nothing.”

Rachel waited. “So what was your belief that your home was better based on?”

Helen looked up. “Nothing.”

The word did not need help. Rachel asked: “Were you threatened into accepting Zoe?”

“No.”

“Did Mac threaten you?”

“No.”

“Did Brian force you?”

“No.”

Helen wiped at one cheek. “I called Mac.”

“You initiated the final acceptance?”

“Yes.”

Her face gave up before her voice did. “I loved her.” Rachel said nothing.

“I know that doesn’t change what I just said.”

“No.”

Helen nodded. “I loved her from the first time I saw her.”

Rachel looked down at her notes. “How did you first see her?”

Helen closed her eyes. “Through glass.”

* * *

Brian remembered the room being too warm. That was one of the first things he told the investigator.

Not the legal process.

Not Mac.

The temperature.

Then the one-way panel.

Zoe on the other side.

Mac refusing to say her parents were dead.

Refusing to say they had given her up.

Brian remembered noticing the resemblance immediately.

The investigator asked:

“Did you believe that resemblance was being used to influence you?”

“Yes.”

“At the time?”

“Yes.”

“Then why did you later agree?”

Brian looked at the table.

“Because I thought if I could see what they were doing, it meant it wasn’t working.”

The investigator stopped writing.

Brian gave a short laugh.

“Stupid.”

“Human.”

He shook his head.

“No. Don’t make it nicer.”

She waited.

“I saw the trick.”

He looked towards the window.

“I still wanted the outcome.”

“You initially wanted to leave.”

“Yes.”

“What changed?”

“Helen called Mac.”

“Did she persuade you?”

“No.”

“You could have refused.”

“Yes.”

“Why didn’t you?”

Brian breathed out.

“Because part of me wanted her to call.”

Another silence.

Then:

“I wanted someone else to be the one who crossed the line.” He had spent five years telling himself Helen was the active one.

Helen reopened the route.

Helen called.

Helen pushed.

All true.

He had still stayed.

Still agreed.

Still paid.

Still reported.

Still protected the arrangement.

The investigator asked:

“Did you understand Zoe may have had an original family with ongoing legal rights?”

“Yes.”

“Did you know who they were?”

“No.”

“Did you try to find out?”

“No.”

“Why?”

Brian stared down at his hands.

“Because I didn’t want an answer that made it harder to keep her.”

The investigator said nothing.

Brian swallowed.

“We told ourselves not knowing was protection.”

“For Zoe?”

He shook his head.

“For us.”

At eleven sixteen, Cain learned Voss and Vale had signed.

He did not have the statements.

Only confirmation that Priya had witnessed two contractor statements and copies were now held outside the old network.

He called Voss.

No answer.

Vale.

Nothing.

Priya.

Voicemail.

Then restricted legal.

The woman answered.

“Have you seen their statements?”

“No.”

“Get them.”

“No.”

Cain closed his eyes.

“Why not?”

“They’re represented.”

“That never stopped you before.”

“Correct.”

Cain stood.

“So now you’re clean?”

“No.”

Her answer came without hesitation.

“I have my own lawyer.”

Cain stopped moving.

“Since when?”

“Since it became obvious I might need one.”

“Who?”

“Independent counsel.”

“You think that protects you?”

“No.”

“Then what are you doing?”

“I’m providing my records.”

Cain said nothing.

“Adult process only. Verification routes. Instructions. Retainers. System records.”

“You give those up and you bury everyone.”

“Maybe.”

“You expose children.”

“No.”

“You don’t control that once it leaves you.”

“Which is why child records are being handled separately.”

“You think families survive this?”

“I don’t know.”

“People go to prison.”

“Yes.”

Cain tightened his grip on the phone.

“Then why?”

A pause.

“Because yesterday a thirteen-year-old girl sat in the back of a car while adults argued over which false authority was allowed to move her.”

Cain said nothing.

“That is what we built.”

“You built paperwork.”

“I built the part that made paperwork beat people.”

No anger.

No raised voice.

Cain said:

“Don’t do this.”

“Too late.”

The call ended.

At eleven forty, Mac entered the office of a solicitor he had never used before.

Independent.

No legacy connection.

He carried one bag.

Paper records.

A USB.

Sponsor agreements.

Invoices.

Recipient introductions.

Payment schedules.

Meridian correspondence.

Messages with restricted legal.

And a sealed envelope.

P06-11803

The solicitor looked at it.

“Zoe?”

“Yes.”

“Child-specific material?”

“Some.”

“Then it stays sealed until we establish how it should be handled.”

Mac almost smiled.

“Good.”

The solicitor pointed at the chair.

“Sit down.”

Mac did.

“Are you here to make a voluntary disclosure?”

“Yes.”

“Do you understand that instructing me does not prevent investigators from acting against you?”

“Yes.”

“Do you understand material you authorise me to provide may incriminate you?”

“Yes.”

“Why are you doing this?”

Mac looked at the sealed envelope.

“Because I can’t control what happens next.”

The solicitor waited.

“That isn’t the whole answer.”

“No.”

“What is?”

Mac rubbed one thumb against the other.

“I spent years calling things protection when what I really meant was keeping control of them.”

Less elegant aloud than it had been in his head.

Probably better.

The solicitor pulled a fresh pad towards him.

“All right.”

The sealed P06 envelope remained unopened.

The placement at the centre of the current crisis could wait until somebody had established who had the right to see it.

* * *

Helen was asked the same final question. Anything else? She almost said it before she could stop herself. “If Zoe ever hears—”

Helen closed her mouth. Of course. Even now she had tried to place herself inside Zoe’s hearing. To explain. To be understood. To say love before the facts had finished. Helen looked back at the recorder. “Nothing further.”

By eight forty-five, Zoe was sitting on the floor beside the bed.

The backpack rested against the wall.

For weeks, losing it had meant losing everything.

Now Voss’s statement existed somewhere else.

So did Vale’s.

North Vale had its own authority contradiction.

Westmere had preserved its own records.

Mac’s signature existed outside the drive.

His payment records were leaving through counsel. Restricted legal was preserving her verification records. Helen had spoken. Brian had spoken. Jackie had given her own account. None of those things depended on Zoe explaining them correctly. None depended on Elias surviving a fight. None depended on one copy of the drive remaining hidden in a backpack.

She looked at Elias. “It’s enough.” He glanced towards the bag. “For what?” “If this disappears.” “The drive?” “Yes.” “Everything?” “No.” She smiled faintly. “That’s the point.” He waited. Zoe looked at the backpack again. Somewhere inside it were still things nobody else had seen. Children who had nothing to do with proving what adults had done.

Lives that did not need to become public just because hers had been invaded.

The truth no longer required all of them.

Elias asked:

“What happens tomorrow?”

Zoe thought about Monday.

About Ruth.

Priya.

The institutions already holding their own records.

The people who had finally put their names beneath what they knew.

She closed the notebook.

“Tomorrow we stop testing.”

And left it there.
