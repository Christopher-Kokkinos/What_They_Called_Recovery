CHAPTER 20 — The First Mistake

On Wednesday morning, Elias taught Zoe how to reverse the farm van.

Not onto a road.

Not through traffic.

Only across the dairy yard, between two chalk lines he had drawn on the concrete.

The van shuddered whenever Zoe released the clutch too quickly. The first time, the engine died so violently that both of them moved forward against their seat belts.

Elias reached for the ignition.

Zoe caught his wrist.

“I know what happened.”

“Do you?”

“I stalled.”

“I was going to restart it.”

“I can restart it.”

He withdrew his hand.

She turned the key, pressed the clutch and started the engine again.

The second attempt carried them backwards six feet before the van stopped at an angle.

“You’re looking at the line,” Elias said.

“That’s where I’m going.”

“Look where the van is going.”

“It is going towards the line.”

“The rear of it isn’t.”

Zoe checked both mirrors.

The left side had drifted towards an old milk churn Elias had placed beside the chalk.

“You put that there deliberately.”

“Yes.”

“It could scratch the van.”

“The van has survived worse.”

She corrected the steering.

By the end of the morning, she could reverse from the machinery shed to the rear gate, turn inside the yard and stop with the driver’s door beside the stone wall.

The distance was less than a hundred metres.

Elias did not teach her how to drive beyond it.

“This is not permission to use the road,” he said.

“I know.”

“It is not a new escape plan.”

“You said that twice.”

“I’m saying it a third time.”

“Because you don’t trust me.”

“Because I trust you to remember the part you prefer.”

She looked at the gate.

“What is it for, then?”

“If the farmhouse is breached while I’m outside, you can move the van across the rear entrance and block it.”

“And then?”

“Leave through the drainage ditch on foot.”

“So the van is not for escaping.”

“It buys time.”

Zoe switched off the engine.

“Everything you teach me buys time.”

“Time is usually what survival costs.”

She removed the key and handed it to him.

Elias closed her fingers around it.

“Keep it.”

Zoe looked down at the key.

“For the lesson?”

“For the farmhouse.”

The distinction was precise.

The van remained his responsibility.

The decision to move it, under the conditions they had rehearsed, became hers.

She placed the key in the same pocket as the second set for the house.

The next four days developed a routine.

Routine did not mean safety.

It meant danger had stopped consuming every available hour.

Zoe rose before Elias on Thursday and found him asleep at the kitchen table with a road map beneath one forearm and a pencil still held between two fingers.

She did not touch him.

She stood in the doorway and said his name once.

His eyes opened immediately.

The pencil broke in his hand.

He looked at the two pieces as if they had disappointed him.

“You said wake you from across the room,” Zoe said.

“I did.”

“You didn’t finish the sentence.”

“What sentence?”

“I don’t know. You were asleep.”

Elias gathered the maps.

One showed the farmhouse and surrounding roads.

Another showed an industrial edge forty minutes away: storage compounds, workshops, a recycling depot, two petrol stations and a market town built around a railway junction.

A small square had been marked beside the freight line.

Zoe recognised the kind of mark.

Not a destination.

A problem isolated from everything around it.

“What is there?”

“A lock-up.”

“Yours?”

“Rented.”

“Under which name?”

“One they may find.”

She looked at him.

“May?”

“They are rebuilding the old identities. Somebody found the cottage through one of them. If they keep following the paper, the lock-up is reachable too.”

“You want her to.”

“I want them to believe they found something I failed to protect.”

Zoe looked at the map again.

“What will be inside?”

“Enough.”

“That isn’t an answer.”

“No.”

He folded the industrial map and put it inside his coat.

For most of the past week, Elias had answered her questions with whatever truth he believed she could use.

This answer had a wall inside it.

Zoe saw it.

She did not challenge it yet.

On Thursday afternoon, they opened the new notebook.

The first three pages contained Zoe’s lessons:

Do not look at the threat. Look at what changes around it.

Trust is also a pattern.

Beneath those, she had written the market-town sequence in order: flowers, café, bus, estate car, newsagent, repaired heel, car park, reservoir, livestock market.

Elias read the account without touching the notebook.

“You left out the chemist.”

“That wasn’t surveillance.”

“It was part of the day.”

Zoe looked at the page.

“The girls?”

“Yes.”

“They don’t matter to the route.”

“No.”

“Then why include them?”

“Because the route is not the whole day.”

She studied him.

He had not asked what shampoo she chose. He had remembered that she had chosen one.

Zoe turned the page.

Three girls in school jumpers.

One wanted to cut her own hair. One said school rules were psychological warfare. They recommended cheaper shampoo because the green one smelled like furniture polish.

She stopped writing.

“That sounds pointless.”

“It may be.”

“You told me detail should perform a function.”

“In operations.”

“What function does this perform?”

Elias looked towards the rain moving down the kitchen window.

“It proves the people hunting you did not own every minute.”

Zoe did not answer.

She underlined the final sentence once.

Friday brought clear weather.

Elias used it to inspect the drainage ditch, the old boundary wall and the footpath leading towards the neighbouring sheep fields.

Zoe followed with the notebook in her coat pocket.

They walked the emergency route twice.

The first ended at a ruined stone shelter half-hidden beneath gorse.

The second continued another mile to a public lane and a red telephone box that no longer contained a telephone.

“That is unhelpful,” Zoe said.

“The village hall is another six hundred metres.”

“Will it be open?”

“No.”

“Also unhelpful.”

“There is a house opposite it with a blue gate. An elderly couple live there. You knock and say there has been an accident at the farm.”

“What accident?”

“You do not invent details. You say you need police and an ambulance.”

“What if they call you?”

“They cannot.”

“What if they ask my name?”

“Zoe.”

“My full name?”

“The one you choose.”

She looked at him.

He did not correct the phrase.

At the stone shelter, Elias had concealed a waterproof bag beneath a loose slab. It contained water, food, a torch, cash, a printed map and a plain coat large enough to cover Zoe’s distinctive backpack.

The bag did not contain a weapon.

She noticed.

“What if someone follows me?”

“You leave the backpack beneath the slab, take only the folder and hard drive under your coat, and continue.”

“You told me never to separate from the bag.”

“I told you to protect what matters.”

“The bag matters.”

“Yes.”

“But less.”

“In that situation.”

Zoe crouched beside the slab.

“Would you leave it?”

“If carrying it made me easier to find.”

“That wasn’t what I asked.”

Elias looked at the faded fabric, the repaired strap and the marks gathered across two countries and fifteen days.

“No,” he said.

Zoe replaced the slab.

“Then don’t ask me to decide quickly.”

“I’m asking you to decide correctly.”

“That is not always the same thing.”

“No.”

They walked back to the farmhouse without resolving it.

The disagreement did not damage the trust between them.

It defined its limit.

Saturday evening, Elias cooked onions badly.

He cut them too thick, burned one side and left the other nearly raw.

Zoe ate around them.

“You could admit they’re ruined,” she said.

“They are caramelised.”

“They are black.”

“Advanced caramelisation.”

She pushed one to the edge of her plate.

“You’re distracted.”

“I’m cooking.”

“Badly.”

“That is not evidence of distraction.”

“It is when the beans were normal.”

Elias looked at the map folded beside the range.

Zoe followed his attention.

“The lock-up.”

He turned the gas down.

“The man who keeps working closest to us is the one I’m watching.”

“You don’t know who he is.”

“No.”

“You don’t know what he’s thinking.”

“No.”

“Then what do you know?”

“At the cottage, one man came close enough for you to study him twice. In town, one man took the exposed position again. When the surveillance changed, he stayed nearest.”

“The flower man.”

“Possibly the same man.”

“You think he’ll come closer again.”

“I think his pattern makes that more likely.”

“That isn’t the same as knowing he will.”

“No.”

Zoe set down her fork.

“You’re using what happened in town.”

“I’m using what he showed us.”

“He knows we saw the surveillance.”

“Yes.”

“That might make him more careful.”

“It might.”

“And if it does?”

“Then the lock-up stays empty and the plan fails.”

Zoe watched him.

“And if it doesn’t?”

“Then somebody may approach before the rest of the team is ready.”

She looked again at the industrial map.

“What happens when he finds the lock-up?”

Elias switched off the gas.

The onions continued to hiss in the pan.

“He believes he has found where we are staying.”

“That still isn’t an answer.”

“No.”

Zoe waited.

Elias moved the pan away from the heat and sat opposite her.

The kitchen smelled of burned onion and paraffin from the lamp.

“I am going there tomorrow night,” he said.

“Alone.”

“Yes.”

“You said nobody enters a confirmed site alone.”

“That was their rule.”

“It was sensible when you said it.”

“It remains sensible.”

“But you are going alone.”

“I am not entering their site.”

“You are making one.”

Elias did not answer.

Zoe understood the shape of the plan before he named it.

The false room.

The discoverable identity.

The man who needed the mistake to belong to someone else.

“You’re going to trap him.”

“Yes.”

The word changed the kitchen.

Not because Zoe had believed Elias incapable of violence. She had seen the service station. She knew what Jackie’s story meant. She had watched men become afraid of his name.

But those moments had been reactions.

This was preparation.

“You said we were trying to lose them,” she said.

“We did.”

“Then why bring one closer?”

“Because losing them is temporary. Mac can replace a trail. Cain can replace watchers. As long as we keep moving away, they choose the pressure.”

“So you kill one.”

“If he comes.”

“That sounds like you’re giving him a choice.”

“He has one.”

“He doesn’t know what it is.”

Elias’s face showed no pleasure in the distinction.

Zoe looked at the notebook.

On the final page she had written every repeated vehicle from the previous three days. None had returned.

“They haven’t found the farmhouse.”

“No.”

“Then this protects us from something that hasn’t happened yet.”

“Yes.”

“Or creates it.”

“Yes.”

He did not reduce the risk to make the plan easier to accept.

That was part of why she believed him.

It was also why the plan frightened her.

“What do I do while you’re gone?”

“Stay here.”

“No.”

“You asked what you do.”

“I’m saying no.”

“You cannot come to the lock-up.”

“I didn’t say I was.”

“Then what are you refusing?”

“To sit here with no information until morning.”

Elias removed a small mechanical timer from his coat and placed it on the table.

It was the kind used for kitchen lights, with a circular dial and plastic pins around the edge.

“I leave at nine,” he said. “I should return before two. If I am not back by four, this alarm sounds.”

“That tells me you didn’t come back.”

“Yes.”

“It doesn’t tell me why.”

“No.”

“What if you’re late?”

“Then you follow the fallback plan.”

“Immediately?”

“You wait ten minutes. Check the yard from upstairs. If nothing has changed, you take the keys, move the van across the rear gate and leave by the drainage ditch.”

“On foot.”

“Yes.”

“To the stone shelter.”

“Yes.”

“And then the blue gate.”

“If you believe the farmhouse has been found.”

“How will I know?”

“Vehicle on the track. Lights where there should be none. Dogs disturbed. A door or window changed. Anything outside the pattern.”

“And if everything is normal?”

“You remain at the shelter until daylight, then go to the blue gate.”

Zoe looked at the timer.

“You planned this without me.”

“I built the first version without you.”

“What version is this?”

“The one you are changing.”

That mattered.

She picked up the timer.

“What if the alarm fails?”

Elias placed a wind-up travel clock beside it.

“Redundancy.”

“What if both fail?”

“You will be awake.”

“You sound confident.”

“You will not sleep.”

She hated that he was right.

Zoe turned the timer over.

“Leave a note.”

“I am telling you now.”

“Leave one anyway.”

Elias nodded.

“And don’t tell me the physical man made his choice if you make sure every part of the room pushes him towards it.”

Elias looked at her.

The sentence found something he had not wanted named.

“I won’t,” he said.

Sunday passed quietly.

Too quietly for Zoe.

Elias repaired the old trail motorcycle stored beneath a tarpaulin in the machinery shed. The tyres were sound. The fuel line was not. He replaced a cracked section, cleaned the plug and started the engine only once, briefly, with the shed doors closed.

The machine was small, dark and older than Zoe.

“You own too many vehicles,” she said.

“I own one.”

“Which?”

“I’m still deciding.”

The motorcycle would take him to the lock-up without removing the farm van from Zoe’s fallback plan.

It also meant nobody watching the known vehicles would see him leave.

At four, Elias slept for ninety minutes.

At six, he ate bread and eggs.

At seven, he showered.

At eight, he placed ordinary clothes on the kitchen chair and changed into darker trousers, a close-fitting jacket and boots without loose metal.

Zoe sat at the table with the notebook closed in front of her.

He removed his wallet, keys and the expired photograph Mac had sent of Zoe.

The photograph remained on the table.

“You’re leaving that,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“It identifies you.”

“So does my face.”

“I am not taking your face.”

“That wasn’t funny.”

“No.”

Elias placed cash in one pocket and a folding knife in another.

No gun.

Zoe noticed that too.

“You don’t use one.”

“Not unless the situation requires distance.”

“You’re planning to be close.”

“Yes.”

The answer made the room colder.

At eight forty-five, Elias walked her through the farmhouse once more.

Front door.

Back door.

Van key.

Alarm.

Drainage ditch.

Shelter.

Blue gate.

He did not repeat the instructions until they became meaningless. He asked questions in the wrong order and made her answer them.

“What do you carry?”

“Folder. Drive. Water. Cash. Map.”

“What do you leave?”

“Everything else if I need to.”

“When do you use the van?”

“To block the rear entrance, not to escape on the road.”

“What if someone tells me you sent them?”

“I don’t believe them.”

“What if they know the plan?”

“I assume they forced it from you or found the note.”

“What if they use my name?”

“Still no.”

“What if they say you’re hurt?”

Zoe looked at him.

“That’s the one you think will work.”

“Yes.”

“It won’t.”

Elias did not say good.

He did not praise her.

He only nodded and accepted that the answer had been given.

At eight fifty-eight, he placed the farmhouse key on the table.

“I have another.”

“I know.”

Zoe stood.

The backpack remained beside her chair.

“Are you coming back?”

“I intend to.”

“That is not a promise.”

“No.”

“Could you make one?”

Elias looked towards the dark window.

“No.”

She appreciated the truth and hated it.

“Then say something useful.”

He thought about it.

“Do not wait past four ten.”

Zoe’s face hardened.

“That is not what I meant.”

“I know.”

He put on his coat.

At the back door, she said his name.

Elias turned.

“You told me trust is a pattern,” Zoe said.

“You wrote that.”

“You agreed with it.”

“Yes.”

“Then whatever happens there becomes part of it.”

Elias stood with one hand on the latch.

“I know.”

He left.

The lock-up occupied the end unit of a storage row behind a tyre depot and a closed furniture warehouse.

At night, the industrial estate reduced itself to geometry.

Roller shutters.

Security lights.

Chain-link fencing.

Concrete lanes carrying the stains of oil and rain.

The railway passed behind the units every forty minutes, announced first by vibration in the metal doors and then by the weight of sound itself.

Elias arrived at ten twelve.

He left the motorcycle in a drainage access beyond the railway embankment and approached on foot.

The lock-up had been rented six years earlier through an identity connected indirectly to the cottage lease.

He had used it twice.

Once to store tools.

Once to disappear for three days after his trainee died.

The second use had left more behind than he realised.

A dent in the inner wall where he had thrown a chair.

A dark mark on the floor beneath a paraffin stove.

A nail above the camp bed where his trainee’s jacket had hung because Elias had not been able to throw it away.

The jacket was gone now.

The nail remained.

Elias had built the false refuge around it.

Camp bed.

Blanket.

Tinned food.

Water.

A cheap electric kettle.

A second-hand coat over a chair.

Mud carried from the farmhouse track and dried near the entrance.

Nothing theatrical.

The room looked occupied because parts of it once had been.

That was the detail no professional staging could manufacture completely.

He had not used anything belonging to Zoe.

No yellow thread.

No schoolbook.

No child’s clothing.

Reyes would come for the evidence, the contract and his own pride.

Elias would not use Zoe as bait even symbolically.

At ten thirty, he checked the adjoining unit.

The old partition between the units had been altered years earlier and could still be crossed from the far side without using the main door.

He checked the route once.

Then waited.

At the farmhouse, Zoe had not opened the notebook.

She had placed it beside the timer, exactly where she could see both without touching either.

At ten forty-seven, headlights moved along the public road beyond the poplars.

Zoe stood but did not go to the window.

She used the reflection in the dark kitchen glass instead.

The vehicle continued.

No turn.

No brake lights.

The sheep in the neighbouring field did not move towards the wall.

She sat again.

At eleven twelve, something struck the machinery-shed roof.

Zoe’s hand closed around the van key.

A second sound followed.

Then a third.

Rain beginning.

She let go.

The farmhouse had rules now.

Not Elias’s rules.

Theirs.

Observe before acting.

Do not make the obvious thing larger than the change around it.

At eleven thirty, the timer still had hours to run.

Zoe checked the loose stone beside the kitchen wall, returned it to the agreed angle and went back inside.

She did not know what Elias was doing.

That uncertainty was the part of the plan he could not rehearse for her.

She remained where they had agreed.

Reyes received the identity bridge at eleven sixteen.

Vale had sent it to the shared channel with a warning:

Possible site. No approach until verified.

The lock-up lease sat two steps beyond the same dormant identity trail that had led them to the cottage: an old correspondence address, a dissolved storage company and a unit rental that had never been formally surrendered.

No current account told them Elias was there.

The paper trail gave them a possible door.

Whether anyone was behind it still had to be established on the ground.

Voss responded first.

Hold position. Twenty-five minutes.

Reyes sat inside the builder’s van three streets away.

Rain marked the windscreen in separate points rather than sheets.

He read the instruction again.

Twenty-five minutes.

By then, Voss would arrive and slow everything down. Vale would inspect the lease history, neighbouring units, access routes and the likelihood that the site had been selected for them.

They would turn one door into an argument lasting half the night.

Reyes looked at the photograph attached to the brief.

Elias.

Tired face.

Ordinary coat.

Driver.

The girl had identified Reyes twice.

At the cottage road.

In the market square.

She had seen through the flowers, the rotation and Vale’s changed bag. Elias had used her observation to walk all three of them through a livestock market and out of the wrong gate.

Voss had revised the assessment.

Vale had called the girl part of the decision process.

Neither had said Reyes failed.

They had not needed to.

The lock-up sat eleven minutes away.

Reyes typed:

Visual only.

He drove.

At eleven thirty-two, the builder’s van entered the industrial estate without headlights for the final hundred metres.

Reyes parked beside the furniture warehouse and approached through the service lane.

He did not walk directly to the lock-up.

He circled the storage row.

Checked the rear fence.

Tested the neighbouring doors.

Looked for fresh tyre marks, camera lenses, wires and disturbed gravel.

He was not incompetent.

He found the motorcycle track near the drainage access.

He found recent mud at the side entrance and warmth leaking from the vent.

A line of light showed beneath the personnel door.

All of it supported occupancy.

None of it established who was inside.

At eleven forty-one, his operational phone vibrated.

Voss.

Reyes silenced it.

He crouched beside the personnel door and examined the lock.

Fresh scratches.

A cheap replacement cylinder.

He checked the lower gap and caught pieces of the room beyond: the edge of a camp bed, a pair of boots, a coat hanging over a chair.

Then something moved beyond his narrow view.

Not enough to identify.

Enough to make waiting feel like losing the site.

He knew Voss was close.

He knew the protocol.

He also knew that another ten minutes could turn a confirmed room into an empty one.

Reyes stood.

His mistake was not entering blind.

It was deciding that the evidence he had gathered justified entering early.

He forced the lock.

The door opened inward.

Reyes entered low and moved immediately away from the frame.

Camp bed.

Coat.

Boots.

No person.

He turned.

Elias came through the adjoining partition.

Reyes blocked the first strike.

His forearm caught Elias across the chest and drove him into the wall hard enough to shake dust from the roof supports.

For several seconds, strength owned the room.

Reyes used it well.

He closed distance and denied Elias space. Elias turned one impact across his shoulder, answered at the ribs and failed to move him.

Reyes smiled.

Not because the fight was easy.

Because at last it was direct.

“You should have kept driving,” he said.

Elias shifted.

Reyes followed.

The movement carried both men into the narrow space beside the workbench.

Reyes lost his footing for less than a second.

Elias used all of it.

The struggle ended on the concrete beneath the old nail.

No speech.

No final revelation.

Only breath becoming irregular, then absent.

The railway passed behind the units.

Metal trembled.

Oil in a shallow tray rippled beneath the workbench.

Elias remained kneeling until the train had gone.

He stood too quickly.

The room shifted.

He placed one hand against the wall and waited for the concrete to become still again.

Reyes lay where the fight had ended.

One sleeve had pulled back from his wrist.

Elias looked away.

The old rhythm had returned exactly as he feared it would.

Assess.

Control.

Finish.

Clear the site.

The body had become an obstacle within seconds.

That was the part that frightened him.

Not that he could still kill.

That his mind still knew how to make killing ordinary enough to continue working around it.

His hands began to shake.

He let them.

Forcing them steady would have transformed the tremor into another task, another problem solved by control.

He stood beside the camp bed until the shaking moved through him and diminished on its own.

Then Reyes’s operational phone vibrated.

The screen lit.

VOSS CALLING

A countdown beneath the name showed the device would auto-lock in forty-three seconds.

Elias picked it up.

The call ended.

Two unread messages appeared.

VOSS: CHECK-IN MISSED. HOLD POSITION.

VALE: Do not breach. Site history incomplete.

The fifteen-minute target-radius protocol had reached its next interval.

The team names sat openly in the thread.

Voss.

Vale.

Reyes.

Elias photographed nothing.

He removed the notebook page folded inside his inner pocket and wrote the names, the last four digits of both contact numbers and the vehicle identifier displayed in the location-sharing panel.

The phone showed three team markers.

Reyes at the lock-up.

Voss approaching from the south.

Vale farther east, moving.

They would find the site within minutes.

Elias powered the phone down before the countdown expired and placed it inside a foil tool pouch taken from the workbench.

He searched Reyes only for items relevant to the pursuit.

Wallet.

Keys.

Operational phone.

No personal phone.

He wiped only what he had introduced unnecessarily, not the fact of his presence.

The scene was not meant to resemble an accident.

Voss and Vale needed to understand that the contract had changed.

Elias left through the adjoining unit.

Headlights entered the far road as he reached the railway embankment.

He remained in shadow until the car passed.

Then he took the motorcycle away from the farmhouse.

For the next forty minutes he used industrial roads, a closed petrol forecourt and two rural junctions to test whether anything stayed with him. He stopped once beneath a railway bridge and listened after the engine died.

Nothing followed.

Only then did he turn west.

He passed the farmhouse road once without using it, continued to the next village and approached again from the opposite direction.

Voss reached the industrial estate after Reyes missed the first fifteen-minute target-radius check-in.

The builder’s van stood beside the furniture warehouse.

No driver.

Voss checked the location feed.

Reyes’s marker remained inside the storage row and had stopped updating.

He called once.

No answer.

He sent the hold instruction again and waited only long enough to confirm there would be no response.

Then he entered the service lane.

The lock-up door stood open.

Voss stopped before the threshold.

From outside he could see the camp bed, the displaced chair, the opened partition and Reyes on the floor.

He did not enter.

He called Vale.

“Confirmed site,” he said.

Her car was six minutes away.

“Reyes?”

Voss watched the body without approaching it.

“Down.”

“Alive?”

“No.”

Vale said nothing for two seconds.

Then:

“Do not touch the phone.”

“It’s gone.”

That changed her breathing.

Not grief.

Calculation arriving through grief because the work did not permit one without the other.

“What else?”

“Keys remain. Wallet remains. Operational phone removed.”

“He knows the identifiers.”

“Yes.”

“Did Reyes breach?”

“Yes.”

“Alone?”

“Yes.”

Vale disconnected.

Voss stood outside the unit and looked at the damaged lock.

Reyes had inspected the approach.

A thin torch rested near the wall.

The lock showed where he had worked on it before forcing entry.

He had not entered blindly.

He had entered early.

There was a difference.

At midnight, Vale arrived.

She parked behind Voss’s car, took one look at his face and did not ask whether he was certain.

Together they documented the approach before crossing the threshold.

Vale saw the room in layers.

The warm vent.

The visible signs of occupation.

The adjoining route.

The real age beneath the staging.

Nothing required Reyes to behave foolishly.

It required him to behave consistently.

That was worse.

A crushed packet of mints showed from the pocket of his coat.

The same brand he had thrown to Voss in Mac’s office.

Voss saw Vale notice it.

Neither of them touched the packet.

The camp bed had been staged, but the wall behind it held older damage. The paraffin mark beneath the stove had soaked into concrete years earlier. One nail remained above the bed at shoulder height.

“This place was real once,” Vale said.

“For Elias?”

“Possibly.”

Voss followed her gaze to the nail.

“He used something true to make the lie hold.”

Vale walked to the partition.

“Reyes saw enough to believe someone was inside. He checked the perimeter. He checked the door.”

“And then entered without us.”

“Because the trap did not attack his competence.”

“It attacked his patience.”

“No.”

Vale looked towards Reyes.

“It attacked the part of him that needed the girl’s success to be temporary.”

Voss removed his glasses and cleaned them with the edge of his shirt, though nothing marked the lenses.

“We should have pulled him after the market.”

“He would not have accepted it.”

“We should have made him.”

Vale looked at Voss.

Voss folded his glasses once, then opened them again.

“Mac gave us a driver,” Vale said.

Voss replaced his glasses.

“No. Mac gave us a description.”

“And paid us not to examine why it was false.”

They stood in the cold unit with Reyes between them.

The contract had not ended.

But its language no longer described what they were doing.

Elias reached the farmhouse shortly after two.

The rear gate remained clear.

No van blocked it.

No light showed through the curtains.

He parked the motorcycle in the machinery shed and approached the house by the drainage ditch to check whether Zoe had followed the route correctly in his absence.

The loose stone beside the kitchen wall remained angled outward.

That was her signal.

Normal inside.

No approach.

Elias replaced it and unlocked the back door.

The kitchen lamp was off.

Zoe sat at the table in darkness with the backpack on her lap and the notebook closed beneath one hand.

The timer showed forty-two minutes remaining.

“You’re early,” she said.

Elias removed his coat.

“By your plan.”

“Not yours?”

“No.”

She looked at his shoulder.

His jacket had torn along one seam. A dark bruise had begun beneath the collar.

“You’re hurt.”

“Not seriously.”

“Did he come?”

“Yes.”

“Alone?”

“Yes.”

Zoe’s fingers tightened around the backpack strap.

Elias placed the foil pouch containing Reyes’s phone on the far end of the table.

She looked at it.

“What is that?”

“His operational phone.”

“You took it.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“It has the other two names and contact channels.”

The explanation confirmed the part she had not asked.

Zoe looked at Elias directly.

“Did you kill him?”

The kitchen offered Elias several ways to avoid the answer.

Operational language.

Necessary force.

He chose none of them. “Yes.”

Zoe’s face did not change immediately. The stillness arrived first. Then she moved the backpack from her lap to the floor. Not closer to Elias. Not farther away. Only somewhere she did not need to hold it while she understood. “Was there another way?” she asked.

“Before tonight?”

“Yes.”

“Probably.”

“Inside the room?”

“No.”

“You designed the room.”

“Yes.”

The answer struck harder than defence would have. Zoe looked at the pouch. “You told me he had a choice.”

“I was wrong.”

“You knew what he would do.”

“I believed I did.”

“And you were right.”

“Yes.”

“That doesn’t make you less wrong.”

“No.”

Elias sat opposite her. His right hand shook once against the table. He withdrew it. Zoe saw. “Are you frightened?”

“Yes.”

“Of the other two?”

“Not at this moment.”

“Of what you did?”

Elias looked towards the dark window.

“Of how quickly it became familiar.”

The words entered the room without protection.

Zoe studied his face.

At the market, he had trusted what she noticed.

Now he was giving her something she might use to leave him.

That was also trust.

It did not make the truth safe.

“Who taught you?” she asked.

“No one person.”

“Who did you teach?”

Elias looked back at her.

The question had not come from nowhere.

She had watched his patience, the way he broke lessons into pieces, the way he anticipated mistakes before she made them.

“You’ve done this before,” Zoe said. “Not the killing. The teaching.”

Elias did not answer.

Zoe opened the notebook to a blank page.

She did not write.

“What was his name?”

The question passed through the room and reached a place the lock-up had already opened.

Elias stood.

“Not tonight.”

“That means there was someone.”

“Yes.”

“What happened?”

“Tomorrow.”

“You told me whatever happened there became part of the pattern.”

“It has.”

“So does this.”

Elias picked up the foil pouch.

The tremor had returned, smaller now.

“Tomorrow,” he repeated.

Zoe watched him carry the dead man’s phone towards the pantry where no signal reached cleanly through the stone walls.

She did not follow.

She did not tell him she trusted him.

She also did not take the farmhouse keys from her pocket and leave.

The night ended with both facts still true.
